Leap to Evidence

From assumptions to interviews

On day one, nearly everything about your business is an assumption: who your customers are, what they need, what they'll pay. You believe these things, but you haven't tested them yet. This tool helps you list your assumptions, decide which to test first, and plan how to test each one, for example with customer discovery interviews.

01Where your idea started

Where your idea started affects which of its assumptions are riskiest.

It started with...
Watch for the Innovator's Bias. When you start from a technology or product, it's easy to describe problems that justify building it. "When you've already decided to build a hammer, everything looks like a nail" (Ash Maurya). Describing more than one business model in step 02 is one check on this bias.

With a platform technology, finding who needs it is its own step. Use Where to Play (Marc Gruber and Sharon Tal; free online tool and worksheets) to find and compare options. Where to Play calls these market opportunities; each one becomes a business model in step 02.

02List your assumptions

An assumption is anything your business needs to be true. Answer the questions below; each answer is an assumption, no matter how sure you feel about it.

Rate each answer honestly. Leap of faith: gut instinct. Anecdote: you heard it from a few people or saw it once. Fact: you have evidence of what many people actually did: many interviews describing the same past behavior, published research, sign-ups, usage data, or sales. No answer yet? Tick "I don't know yet." Interviews and market research will help you form one.

Your business models

A business model is your full set of answers for one customer with one problem. If you change the customer or the problem, most of the other answers change too, so that's a new business model. If you're weighing two options for the same customer and problem, such as two prices or two ways to reach them, don't start a new business model. Add both options as answers to that question.

Questions combine the Lean Canvas (Ash Maurya) and the Business Model Canvas (Alexander Osterwalder), with rules and approvals from David J. Bland's Assumptions Mapping Worksheet (Precoil).

03Your testing order

Calculated from your answers, not from how important things feel.

How the order is calculated

Consequence. If a technology assumption fails, every business model fails: Stop. If a customer or problem assumption fails, the tool checks your other business models. If at least one doesn't depend on it: Pivot. If all of them do: Stop. Money assumptions (price, channels, costs) are judged with a money model in stage 2.

Dependencies. How many of your other assumptions depend on this one. On the canvas, later boxes depend on earlier ones: problems belong to a customer, prices depend on the problem, channels depend on who the customer is.

Evidence. Leap-of-faith assumptions go before anecdotes; facts go last.

Earlier links first. If an earlier assumption is still a leap of faith, a "no" on a later assumption could mean only that the earlier assumption was wrong, so the later assumption waits. Customer and problem assumptions are tested together, and technology tests run alongside interviews.

    Check this order with an advisor or coach. Before you interview customers, show your business models and this list to someone with experience in your industry or in starting companies. In step 05, choose "Advisor or coach" for a conversation plan.

    When to stop

    In qualitative research, interviews with similar people stop producing new issues after 9 to 17 interviews, and understanding those issues in depth takes more interviews (Hennink, Kaiser and Marconi, 2017; Hennink and Kaiser, 2022). Count only interviews with people who match the customer in this business model and play the role you chose above. For example, coffee shop owners who handle their own invoices, interviewed as end users; their bookkeepers count separately. Recruit them from different places.
